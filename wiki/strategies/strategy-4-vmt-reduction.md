## Foundation

Strategy 4 of CAP-2020 targets reducing vehicle miles traveled by at least 50%, with the seven combined actions projected to reduce just over 8% of community-wide emissions at a cost of just over $901,000,000, driven mainly by physical infrastructure costs [Source: Page 69] ([[sources/cap/cap-2020|cap-2020]]). The dominant mechanisms span land-use policy, transit investment, and pricing tools, including non-motorized infrastructure buildout, local and regional bus rapid transit expansion, park-and-ride expansion, mixed-use and housing-diversity zoning updates, and tiered/dynamic parking rates. The seven named actions are: (1) [[initiatives/non-motorized-transportation-plan|Implement Non-Motorized Transportation Plan]], (2) [[initiatives/expand-improve-local-transit|Expand and Improve Local Transit]], (3) [[initiatives/expand-improve-regional-transit|Expand and Improve Regional Transit]], (4) [[initiatives/park-and-ride-expansion|Increase Number of Park and Rides and Ensure Seamless Connection to Transit]], (5) [[initiatives/increase-diversity-of-housing|Increase Diversity of Housing]], (6) Establish Mixed-Use Neighborhoods, and (7) Tiered Parking Rates [Source: Page 69] ([[sources/cap/cap-2020|cap-2020]]). Of the actions with independently quantified costs and reductions, Expand and Improve Local Transit is the largest single contributor, projected at 4.3% of community-wide emissions (93,200 MTCO₂e) at a cost of $656,780,000, while several other actions ([[initiatives/expand-improve-regional-transit|Expand and Improve Regional Transit]], [[initiatives/develop-mixed-use-neighborhoods|Develop Mixed-Use Neighborhoods]], and [[initiatives/tiered-parking-rates|Establish Tiered Parking Rates]]) note that their emissions reduction potential is captured within other actions ([[sources/cap/cap-2020|cap-2020]]).

## Progress Synthesis

Before year four, Strategy 4 had already combined infrastructure delivery, transit funding, and land-use reform. Year three included 3.1 miles of new or improved bike lanes, 2.5 miles of sidewalk-gap filling, new crosswalks, a downtown no-turn-on-red resolution, passage of a transit-supporting millage for [[actors/ann-arbor-area-transit-authority|TheRide]], approximately 400 acres rezoned to TC1 transit-corridor status, and launch of a Comprehensive Plan update with carbon neutrality and equity as priorities. [[locations/ann-arbor|Ann Arbor]] also received Gold-level walk-friendly and bicycle-friendly recognition during that period. ([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year3|a2zero-year3]])

In year four, the city continued pursuing the same theory of change: reduce driving by making walking, biking, transit, and compact land use more practical and attractive. The city funded or supported 12.1 miles of bike facilities and 21 bike intersection improvements, filled 1.39 miles of sidewalk gaps, installed 13 new crosswalks, and added two new audible pedestrian signals. It also launched dashboards to monitor sidewalk-gap replacement, traffic calming, and Vision Zero progress. Shared mobility and transit-supportive systems expanded as well, including a reintroduced micromobility system with scooters and e-bikes that logged more than 214,000 trips in 2023. Land-use reform remained a major enabling condition through the comprehensive land use plan update, elimination of car parking minimums in most developments, and creation of proposed zoning districts for transit corridors. The report did not provide a new citywide VMT outcome figure, so progress remained best understood as enabling-condition progress rather than direct evidence that driving had fallen at the pace needed to meet the long-term target. ([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year4|a2zero-year4]])

Year five adds less new quantitative transportation data than some other strategies, but it reinforces the same implementation logic: land-use reform and non-motorized infrastructure are the main levers OSI is directly shaping. The report says OSI's primary emphasis under Strategy 4 was supporting development of the city's draft Comprehensive Land Use Plan, with sustainability named as one of the plan's three core tenets, and it notes that public comment is open for those wanting to share feedback. That suggests the city increasingly sees VMT reduction as something embedded in growth patterns and development rules, not only in transportation projects. ([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year5|a2zero-year5]])

The city also continued funding and collaborating on bike lanes, crosswalks, better lighting, bike parking expansion, and related features to improve non-motorized mobility options. In collaboration with [[actors/human-electric-hybrids|Human Electric Hybrids]] and [[actors/bicycle-alliance-of-washtenaw|Bicycle Alliance of Washtenaw]], the city launched the "[[initiatives/borrow-a-cargo-bike-a2|Borrow a Cargo Bike A2]]" program, and OSI also purchased its own cargo bike for carrying materials to public engagement events. The city continued updating dashboards on sidewalk-gap replacements, traffic calming, and Vision Zero progress, and again highlighted Ann Arbor's Gold-level recognition from Walk Friendly Communities and Gold-level Bicycle Friendly Community designation from the League of American Bicyclists. The report still does not provide a new citywide VMT reduction figure, so the year-five synthesis remains one of institutional alignment, infrastructure expansion, and mobility-option building rather than demonstrated emissions outcomes from reduced driving. ([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year5|a2zero-year5]])
