## Foundation

Strategy 1 of CAP-2020 targets powering [[locations/ann-arbor|Ann Arbor]]'s entire electrical grid with 100% renewable energy through four named actions: Implement [[initiatives/community-choice-aggregation|Community Choice Aggregation]], Support Onsite Renewables and Battery Storage, Develop [[initiatives/community-solar-program|Community Solar Program]], and Launch [[initiatives/landfill-solar-project|Landfill Solar Project]] ([[sources/cap/cap-2020|cap-2020]]). Combined, these four actions are projected to reduce community-wide greenhouse gas emissions by "41%" at a cost of "just over $4,100,000" [Source: Page 21] ([[sources/cap/cap-2020|cap-2020]]). The dominant policy mechanism is Community Choice Aggregation (CCA), an opt-out bulk-purchasing program through which the City procures 100% renewable electricity on behalf of residents, businesses, and municipal accounts from an alternative supplier, contingent on enabling state legislation ([[sources/cap/cap-2020|cap-2020]]). The remaining three actions support local generation through discounted bulk-buy programs for onsite solar and battery storage (targeting 78 MW by 2030), a community solar program (targeting 11 MW by 2030), and a 24 MW utility-scale landfill solar project developed in partnership with [[actors/dte-energy|DTE Energy]] ([[sources/cap/cap-2020|cap-2020]]).

## Progress Synthesis

Before year four, Strategy 1 had already developed along several fronts: distributed solar deployment, utility regulatory intervention, and feasibility analysis for longer-term grid transformation. By the end of year three, [[initiatives/solarize-ann-arbor|Solarize Ann Arbor]] had surpassed 4 MW of rooftop solar through more than 540 residential installations, a [[initiatives/commercial-solar-program|Commercial Solar Program]] had launched with the [[actors/ann-arbor-washtenaw-2030-district|Ann Arbor / Washtenaw 2030 District]], and a joint RFP with the [[actors/university-of-michigan|University of Michigan]] sought 1 MW of solar for city facilities and up to 20 MW at university sites. OSI also led broader dissemination of the Solarize model, served on the national SolSmart Advisory Committee, and continued utility-regulatory engagement through [[actors/michigan-public-service-commission|Michigan Public Service Commission]] proceedings. ([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year3|a2zero-year3]])

In year four, the strategy moved further into visible deployment. The city installed more than 250 kW of rooftop and ground-mounted solar at [[locations/leslie-park-golf-course|Leslie Park Golf Course]], [[locations/huron-hills-golf-course|Huron Hills Golf Course]], [[locations/cobblestone-farm|Cobblestone Farm]], [[locations/buhr-park|Buhr Park]], [[locations/ann-arbor-senior-center|Ann Arbor Senior Center]], [[locations/gallup-canoe-livery|Gallup Canoe Livery]], and [[locations/fuller-park|Fuller Park]], while actively designing nearly 4 MW more for the [[locations/ann-arbor-municipal-airport|Municipal Airport]], [[locations/ann-arbor-municipal-center|Ann Arbor Municipal Center]], [[locations/wheeler-center|Wheeler Service Center]], [[locations/water-resource-recovery-facility|Water Resource Recovery Facility]], [[locations/veterans-memorial-park|Veterans Memorial Park]], [[locations/fire-station-3|Fire Station 3]], [[locations/bicentennial-park|Bicentennial Park]], and [[locations/fire-station-6|Fire Station 6]], with deployment expected in year five. The city also secured $2.5 million in federal support for additional solar and storage, began work on a 500 kW battery at the [[locations/water-resource-recovery-facility|Water Resource Recovery Facility]] paired with rooftop solar, applied for and secured funding for solar panels at the new election center, and continued re-scoping the [[initiatives/landfill-solar-project|Wheeler Center/former landfill solar effort]], which received a $5,000,000 award to advance solar at and around the site. Community-scale adoption also accelerated: in calendar year 2023, [[initiatives/solarize-ann-arbor|Solarize Ann Arbor]] supported 231 solar installations totaling more than 1.8 MW, bringing cumulative deployment to over 4.8 MW and more than 630 households served. OSI also published "Advancing Sustainability with Your MUD or HOA" and created a sample HOA sustainability policy. ([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year4|a2zero-year4]])

Year four also widened the city's utility and resilience options. OSI secured a $70,000 grant to design a [[technology/microgrid|microgrid]] at [[locations/veterans-memorial-park|Veterans Memorial Park]] and [[locations/fire-station-3|Fire Station 3]], submitted a federal grant to finalize that design and install the microgrid, drafted community choice aggregation legislation, supported state microgrid legislation, and launched community education around the [[initiatives/sustainable-energy-utility|Sustainable Energy Utility]] after completing a technical study and rate analysis. Voters were scheduled to decide in November 2024 whether to formally create the SEU. OSI also shared results from the [[initiatives/100-renewable-energy-pathways-study|100% Renewable Energy Pathways Study]], opened public comment on the [[initiatives/municipalization-study-phase-ii|Phase II Municipalization Study]] authorized by Council in May 2024, and submitted testimony in multiple MPSC cases. The report tied these local actions to external constraints and opportunities, including DTE's fuel mix and Michigan's new clean-energy law. The strategy also intersected with building decarbonization through support for geothermal at [[locations/121-catherine-street|121 Catherine Street]], funding for the [[locations/bryant-neighborhood-community-center|Bryant Community Center]] to become the city's first fully carbon-neutral building and a living, learning laboratory, and initiation of a citywide networked geothermal study. ([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year4|a2zero-year4]])

Year five shows this strategy shifting from diversified exploration into more explicit local-energy-system buildout. The biggest political milestone was voter authorization of the [[initiatives/sustainable-energy-utility|Sustainable Energy Utility]] on November 5, 2024, with 79% support. OSI then launched an SEU waitlist, began sending nearly biweekly updates, and secured $5,000,000 to support startup work. Solar deployment also continued: [[initiatives/solarize-ann-arbor|Solarize Ann Arbor]] reached 5.4 MW installed through the program; the city installed 700 kW of new solar on municipal properties, bringing total city-property generating capacity above 1 MW; and additional solar was designed for Bicentennial Park, Fire Station 1, City Hall, the Election Center, Wheeler Service Center, Steere Farm, the Water Resource Recovery Facility, and more. The city also advanced battery storage at the Water Resource Recovery Facility and Fire Station 6, supported the groundbreaking for Ann Arbor's first net zero energy fire station on May 20, and applied for Direct Pay rebates for solar, vehicle, and energy-storage systems already put into operation, leading to nearly $320,000 in direct rebates. ([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year5|a2zero-year5]])

The strategy's dependence on external utility conditions remains explicit. The report says that in 2024 over 40% of DTE's electric fuel mix used to supply electricity in Ann Arbor was generated using coal, compared to 13% from renewables. At the same time, through collaboration with [[actors/constellation-energy|Constellation Energy]] and enrollment of 67 additional city sites in Michigan's Electric Customer Choice Program, over 70% of the electricity used to power city facilities and operations is now generated by renewable energy sources. OSI also continued state-level advocacy by drafting community choice aggregation legislation, supporting microgrid legislation, serving on the national SolSmart Advisory Committee, intervening for the first time in two gas cases before the MPSC while calling for a "Future of Gas" proceeding, and participating in reliability and distributed-energy-resource working groups. Geothermal also became more central to the strategy's energy-system framing: OSI and collaborators won $10,000,000 to support deployment of a networked geothermal system in the [[locations/bryant-neighborhood|Bryant neighborhood]], launched an 18-month citywide geothermal feasibility analysis with [[actors/stantec|Stantec]], integrated geothermal into the affordable housing site on Catherine Street, and installed geothermal at the Bryant Community Center. In the report's greenhouse-gas summary, the DOE-backed geothermal project is described as designed to help 262 homes, a community center, and a local school transition away from fossil gas. ([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year5|a2zero-year5]])
