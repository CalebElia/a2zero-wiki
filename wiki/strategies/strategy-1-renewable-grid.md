## Foundation

Strategy 1 of CAP-2020 targets powering Ann Arbor's entire electrical grid with 100% renewable energy through four named actions: Implement Community Choice Aggregation, Support Onsite Renewables and Battery Storage, Develop [[initiatives/community-solar-program|Community Solar Program]], and Launch Landfill Solar Project ([[sources/cap/cap-2020|cap-2020]]). Combined, these four actions are projected to reduce community-wide greenhouse gas emissions by "41%" at a cost of "just over $4,100,000" [Source: Page 21] ([[sources/cap/cap-2020|cap-2020]]). The dominant policy mechanism is Community Choice Aggregation (CCA), an opt-out bulk-purchasing program through which the City procures 100% renewable electricity on behalf of residents, businesses, and municipal accounts from an alternative supplier, contingent on enabling state legislation ([[sources/cap/cap-2020|cap-2020]]). The remaining three actions support local generation through discounted bulk-buy programs for onsite solar and battery storage (targeting 78 MW by 2030), a community solar program (targeting 11 MW by 2030), and a 24 MW utility-scale landfill solar project developed in partnership with [[actors/dte-energy|DTE Energy]] ([[sources/cap/cap-2020|cap-2020]]).

## Progress Synthesis

Before year four, Strategy 1 had already developed along several fronts: distributed solar deployment, utility regulatory intervention, and feasibility analysis for longer-term grid transformation. By the end of year three, [[initiatives/solarize-ann-arbor|Solarize Ann Arbor]] had surpassed 4 MW of rooftop solar through more than 540 residential installations, a [[initiatives/commercial-solar-program|Commercial Solar Program]] had launched with the 2030 District, and a joint RFP with the [[actors/university-of-michigan|University of Michigan]] sought 1 MW of solar for city facilities and up to 20 MW at university sites. OSI also led broader dissemination of the Solarize model, served on the national SolSmart Advisory Committee, and continued utility-regulatory engagement through Michigan Public Service Commission proceedings (([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year3|a2zero-year3]])).

In year four, the strategy moved further into visible deployment. The city installed more than 250 kW of rooftop and ground-mounted solar at [[locations/leslie-park-golf-course|Leslie Park Golf Course]], [[locations/huron-hills-golf-course|Huron Hills Golf Course]], [[locations/cobblestone-farm|Cobblestone Farm]], [[locations/buhr-park|Buhr Park]], [[locations/ann-arbor-senior-center|Ann Arbor Senior Center]], [[locations/gallup-canoe-livery|Gallup Canoe Livery]], and [[locations/fuller-park|Fuller Park]], while actively designing nearly 4 MW more for the [[locations/ann-arbor-municipal-airport|Municipal Airport]], [[locations/ann-arbor-municipal-center|Ann Arbor Municipal Center]], [[locations/wheeler-service-center|Wheeler Service Center]], [[locations/water-resource-recovery-facility|Water Resource Recovery Facility]], [[locations/veterans-memorial-park|Veterans Memorial Park]], [[locations/fire-station-3|Fire Station 3]], [[locations/bicentennial-park|Bicentennial Park]], and [[locations/fire-station-6|Fire Station 6]], with deployment expected in year five. The city also secured $2.5 million in federal support for additional solar and storage, began work on a 500 kW battery at the Water Resource Recovery Facility paired with rooftop solar, applied for and secured funding for solar panels at the new election center, and continued re-scoping the [[initiatives/landfill-solar-project|Wheeler Center/former landfill solar effort]], which received a $5,000,000 award to advance solar at and around the site (([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year4|a2zero-year4]])).

Community-scale adoption also accelerated. In calendar year 2023, [[initiatives/solarize-ann-arbor|Solarize Ann Arbor]] supported 231 solar installations totaling more than 1.8 MW, the program's highest annual installation count, bringing cumulative deployment to over 4.8 MW and more than 630 households served. The report says Solarize savings last year topped $296,000 in upfront cost savings and are estimated at $2.4 million in energy-cost savings over the 25-year life of the systems installed through the program. To broaden access in multi-unit settings, OSI published "Advancing Sustainability with Your MUD or HOA" and created a sample HOA sustainability policy for use in the city or state (([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year4|a2zero-year4]])).

Year four also widened the city's utility and resilience options. OSI secured a $70,000 grant to design a [[technology/microgrid|microgrid]] at [[locations/veterans-memorial-park|Veterans Memorial Park]] and [[locations/fire-station-3|Fire Station 3]], submitted a federal grant to finalize that design and install the microgrid, drafted [[initiatives/community-choice-aggregation|community choice aggregation]] legislation, supported state microgrid legislation, and launched community education around the [[initiatives/sustainable-energy-utility|Sustainable Energy Utility]] after completing a technical study and rate analysis. Voters were scheduled to decide in November 2024 whether to formally create the SEU. OSI also shared results from the [[initiatives/100-renewable-energy-pathways-study|100% Renewable Energy Pathways Study]], opened public comment on the [[initiatives/municipalization-study-phase-ii|Phase II Municipalization Study]] authorized by Council in May 2024, and submitted testimony in MPSC cases U21291, U-21172, U-21384, U-21376, U-21534, and U-21400. The report ties these local actions to external constraints and opportunities: DTE's 2023 fuel mix remained more than 50% coal and 13% renewable, while Michigan's new law requires the state to generate 50% of its total electricity from clean energy sources by 2030 and 100% by 2040. The strategy also intersected with building decarbonization through support for geothermal at [[locations/121-catherine-street|121 Catherine Street]], funding for the [[locations/bryant-neighborhood-community-center|Bryant Community Center]] to become the city's first fully carbon-neutral building and a living, learning laboratory, and initiation of a citywide networked geothermal study. Year five priorities include reaching 5.5 MW through Solarize and installing solar, or having a plan to install solar, at every viable city facility (([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year4|a2zero-year4]])).
