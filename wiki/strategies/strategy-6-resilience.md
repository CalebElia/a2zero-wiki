## Foundation

Strategy 6 of CAP-2020 targets a combined 0.1% reduction in community-wide greenhouse gas emissions at a total cost of approximately $7,500,000 [Source: Page 99] ([[sources/cap/cap-2020|cap-2020]]). The dominant mechanism is community resilience-building rather than direct emissions reduction, with the strategy explicitly noting that reducing greenhouse gas emissions was not the primary selection criterion for its actions. The strategy comprises six named actions: Invest in Resilience Hubs, Foster Neighborhood and Youth Ambassador Programs, [[initiatives/preserve-and-enhance-local-tree-canopy|Preserve and Enhance the Local Tree Canopy]], [[initiatives/asset-and-needs-mapping-of-neighborhoods|Conduct Asset and Needs Mapping of Neighborhoods]], [[initiatives/emergency-preparedness-kits|Assist in Assembling and Distributing Emergency Preparedness Kits]], and [[initiatives/environmental-sensors-monitoring|Implement Sensors to Monitor and Manage Heat, Air Quality, Waterways, and Flooding]] [Source: Page 99] ([[sources/cap/cap-2020|cap-2020]]).

## Progress Synthesis

Before year four, Strategy 6 had already established resilience hubs, tree-canopy expansion, and environmental monitoring as core implementation areas. Year three included completion of the second resilience hub at the [[locations/bryant-neighborhood-community-center|Bryant Neighborhood Community Center]], operational use of the [[initiatives/northside-resilience-hub|Northside Community Center Resilience Hub]] during a February ice storm, an EPA grant application to expand a countywide resilience network, growth of the [[initiatives/10000-trees-initiative|10,000 Trees Initiative]] to roughly 5,750 trees, expansion of the air-quality monitoring network to four sensors, launch of a public air-data platform, and growth of the [[initiatives/a2zero-ambassadors-program|A2Zero Ambassadors Program]] to 85 members (([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year3|a2zero-year3]])).

In year four, resilience expanded from a city-site model toward a broader neighborhood and regional network. The city won a $1,000,000 [[actors/us-epa|U.S. EPA]] Environmental Justice Government-to-Government grant to launch the [[initiatives/model-regional-resilience-network|Model Regional Resilience Network]] and expand [[initiatives/resilience-hubs|resilience hubs]] in Ann Arbor and across [[actors/washtenaw-county|Washtenaw County]]. [[locations/peace-neighborhood-center|Peace Neighborhood Center]] and the [[locations/green-baxter-court-community-center|Green Baxter Court Community Center]] were identified as the next two resilience hub sites, with initial engagement and planning underway. The city also established the [[locations/bryant-neighborhood-community-center|Bryant Community Center]] as its second resilience hub (([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year4|a2zero-year4]])).

Backup power and distributed energy became more central to the resilience model. The city purchased a [[technology/mobile-nanogrid|mobile nanogrid]] for sustainability education and emergency backup power, conducted two pilot battery-backup Solarize bulk buys that resulted in 37 battery systems and $101,650 in upfront savings, increased solar-and-storage capacity at the Bryant Community Center so it can provide three days of power with no grid connection, and added battery backup at the [[locations/ann-arbor-senior-center|Ann Arbor Senior Center]]. OSI and the [[actors/office-of-emergency-management|Office of Emergency Management]] also submitted a [[actors/fema|FEMA]] BRIC application for a microgrid at [[locations/fire-station-3|Fire Station 3]] and [[locations/veterans-memorial-park|Veterans Memorial Park]] (([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year4|a2zero-year4]])).

Natural systems, stewardship, and preparedness programs also advanced. The [[initiatives/10000-trees-initiative|10,000 Trees Initiative]] reached 8,903 trees planted or distributed and 2,862 recipients. A $1,000,000 [[actors/usda-forest-service|U.S. Forest Service]] grant was won with [[actors/forestry-and-natural-areas-preservation|Forestry and Natural Areas Preservation]] for a comprehensive canopy analysis, tree planting and maintenance in underserved neighborhoods, and stewardship work in natural areas. The city developed a survival study for planted trees, continued air-quality monitoring while addressing equipment reliability issues, launched an air-quality visualization map, ordered six additional monitors for deployment, and expanded the [[initiatives/pollinator-aware-yard-care|Pollinator-Aware Yard Care Program]] to 396 registered addresses with 332 yard signs distributed. It also continued adoption of [[locations/bandemer-park|Bandemer Park]] through monthly workdays focused on litter cleanup, native planting, invasive removal, and facility maintenance (([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year4|a2zero-year4]])).

Community preparedness and ambassador-linked resilience work also grew, though the detailed ambassador and communications infrastructure sits more centrally under Strategy 7. In year four, the fifth and sixth ambassador cohorts brought the total trained to 100. Ambassadors participated in science communication training, helped launch [[initiatives/green-energy-neighbors|Green Energy Neighbors]], supported a rain garden at the [[locations/genesis-building|Genesis Building]], and helped host the [[initiatives/nourishing-neighborhood-pilot|Nourishing Neighborhood Pilot]] in the [[locations/darlington-neighborhood|Darlington Neighborhood]]. OSI also supported winter-preparedness tabletop planning with the City Clerk's Office, hosted an emergency-kit distribution and household preparedness seminar at [[actors/washtenaw-community-college|Washtenaw Community College]], and joined [[actors/avalon-housing|Avalon Housing]]'s emergency-preparedness working group. Year five priorities include creating the third and fourth resilience hubs and achieving A2ZERO goals for trees, emergency kits, ambassadors, and air-quality monitoring (([[sources/annual-reports/a2zero-year4|a2zero-year4]])).
